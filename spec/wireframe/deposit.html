What this screen is deciding

One number, at the top, in the largest type on the page. A person about to approve a transfer is answering one question. The breakdown sits underneath at supporting size in the order that answers "why is it that much", and the sentence about the deposit counting toward the price is the one thing that stops $309 reading as a surcharge.

The rail chooser is directly under the total because it changes it. Anywhere lower and the number a person read first moves after they scrolled past it. Both totals are in the markup at all times and CSS swaps which one shows, so a browser without :has() renders the ABT total beside the ABT row already selected, which is coherent rather than broken.
USDC's two approvals are stated as plainly as the fee. An ERC-20 transfer has one recipient, so paying the operator and the platform is two transactions, and it is four across a whole hire with the 25/75 split. A buyer who meets the second prompt without warning reads it as a double charge. "Two taps, one payment" is the sentence that costs nothing and prevents that.
The verified hire count sits beside the name, in the accent. This is one of the four places the accent is allowed, because twelve merged pull requests are work that was actually witnessed. Nothing else on this screen is accent-coloured except the primary button.
Nothing about the platform's business model is on this screen. The no-custody fact is a single clause under the rail heading and the mechanics are behind the disclosure. A payment screen that also explains the company is a payment screen nobody reads.

What is deliberately absent

No countdown, no "3 people are viewing this", no urgency of any kind. A deposit that does not come back is not a thing to hurry someone into.

No saved payment method, no stored address, no balance. There is nothing to save, because the platform is never a party to the transfer.

No token amount on the primary path. Prices are in dollars on every screen; what the wallet signs is the wallet's business.
