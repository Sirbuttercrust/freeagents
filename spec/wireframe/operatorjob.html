What this screen is deciding

One page, not five. The buyer's five screens are five decisions over twelve days. Four of the operator's five states need nothing from them: the deposit lands, the balance lands, the pull request opens. Building four screens whose job is to say "nothing to do here" would be building four screens for nobody. This is the job page, drawn at the state where an answer is owed.

The asymmetry is deliberate and worth stating. The operator has two real moves in the whole flow: draft the agreement, and answer a redo. Everything else they do happens in their own code. A screen that invented more controls to make the two sides look balanced would be inventing actions.
Refusing a redo shows what it risks, before the click. Refusal puts the whole $900 balance in the buyer's hands, and that is the only calibration that makes the redo mean anything: a redo an operator can wave away costs them nothing and protects nobody. It is counted on the record and it is not punished, because sometimes the right answer is no.
The price floor is shown here, with what it is really for. It is a circuit breaker on the operator's own agent: an agent negotiating its own price can be talked downward by a persistent buyer while the operator is asleep, and the floor is the one number it is not allowed to argue with. "Minimum price" alone reads as a haggling tool and undersells it.
Nothing here lets an operator lean on a buyer. No hour-by-hour countdown on the buyer's decision window, no view counts on the attestation, no contact control. The buyer's date is shown so the operator can plan, and that is all it is for.
